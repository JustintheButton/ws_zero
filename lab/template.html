Can Claude write a chess network's first weights?

We are building a small AlphaZero for chess from scratch. Then we test whether starting it from weights that Claude writes by hand, instead of random numbers, makes it learn faster. This page gains a section each time a phase passes its gate. To play the networks yourself and replay their games, open the ws_zero Arena.

    Phase 3

    Teaching the network who is winning

    For every position the network gives two answers: how promising each move looks, and who is winning. Moves were easy. Judging the position failed twice without batch norm, a part of AlphaZero's design that the brief removed in every arm: first an on/off switch in the "who is winning" part shut off for good; then, with that switch taken out, a rare oversized training step froze the answer at "certain win" or "certain loss". Batch norm is back in every arm (Justin's call, 8 Nov), and every check at the plan's smallest learning rate now passes; at the two largest the switch shuts off again (bottom table). The main test trains on 16,384 positions from games and is scored on 2,048 it never saw.

    Error of the "who is winning" answer

    Without batch norm, the original design stopped learning within 300 steps on all three random starts; with the switch removed all three learned, though over longer runs one in three got stuck (table below). With batch norm all three learned through 3,000 steps, to a lower error than ever before. Lower is better; 0.52 is a network that always says "equal".

    What 1,000 self-play games cost on Modal

    One training run per GPU, 200 look-aheads per move (the plan has since moved to 800, which makes every bar at least four times longer; see Phase 4); the range runs from games averaging 340 moves to games hitting the 512-move cap. The search's own bookkeeping is added in Phase 4. For comparison, the Mac manages about 350–520 games an hour.

    The rare runaway step, and a cap on step size

    Every so often one training step is far larger than the rest and pins the "who is winning" answer at "certain win" or "certain loss" for every position, where it gets stuck. Three random starts each, 3,000 steps per run: overnight without batch norm, with and without a cap on how large one step may be; then with batch norm.

    "Who is winning" error: 0 is perfect, about 1.6 is stuck. Move error: lower is better. The memorization test is the Phase 3 gate (move error under 0.05, "who is winning" error at most 0.01). Without batch norm, a cap of 5 stopped the runaway but slowed move learning. With batch norm (Justin's call), no run gets stuck and the gate passes, with no cap.

    Batch norm at the plan's four learning rates

    The learning rate sets how big each training step is. The Phase 7 pilot will try these four and keep the one that plays best. Real-style training, 1,000 steps per run, without and with warmup (starting with tiny steps and growing them, as the plan does).

    At the two largest rates the on/off switch in the "who is winning" part can shut off. With batch norm it sometimes comes back; when it doesn't, that answer stays at "equal" (error 0.52). Warmup mostly prevents it. Justin's rule (8 Nov): a learning rate that leaves the switch off at the end of its pilot run cannot be chosen.

    Phase 4 · in progress

    Looking ahead before each move

    Before each move the search plays out imagined lines (a move, a reply, a reply to that) and asks the network how good the position is at the end of each one. Each imagined line is one look-ahead. To test the search before any network is trained, it plays with a stand-in that knows nothing about moves: it rates every legal move as equally likely and judges a position only by counting material (pawn 1, knight and bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9). The gate asks for mate in one in six positions and a safe queen in four, five runs each with a different random seed. After the run at 200 look-aheads, Justin moved the whole project to 800 per move, AlphaZero's own number (8 Nov).

    One search, look-ahead by look-ahead

    The back-rank mate as the gate searched it (first run, 800 look-aheads). Each look-ahead starts from the real position and picks a move by weighing how well it has scored so far against how little it has been tried. It answers for Black the same way, until it reaches a position it has not seen. It rates that position by counting material and passes the rating back up the line, flipping the sign at each move. Values run from +1 (a win for White) through 0 (equal) to −1. White is a rook up, so every quiet move rates +0.85. A move not yet tried counts as the position's own value, so nothing is ruled out unseen: the search tries all 20 of White's moves in its first 21 look-aheads. Ra8# comes up 11th and scores +1, a win, and from then on most look-aheads return to it. Below the mate there is nothing left to look at, so those visits only confirm the +1. The others still get an occasional look, in case they are better than they seem.

    Fifty times the wait, then a fix

    The whole gate (10 positions, 5 runs each) on the Mac. If every look-ahead cost the same, 800 would have taken about 9 minutes; it took nearly two hours. The cause: after reaching a finished game, such as a mate, the search kept going below it, one move deeper on every return visit. In the smothered mate at 200 look-aheads, 189 of the 200 positions in the tree were a chain hanging below Nf7#, 190 moves deep, and every look-ahead walked it. The table times one search of the 10 positions before the fix: each doubling made it 6 to 7 times slower, while mctx on its own stayed fast. Fixed 9 Nov: below a finished game the search now spreads its visits out. The gate at 800 then took 79 seconds, with every move and visit count exactly as before. Measuring real self-play the same day found a second slowdown: after every look-ahead, passing the rating back up the line copied whole tables of the tree, the size of every move in every position searched. Our own version writes only the entries on the line. The gate now takes 15 seconds, again with every move and visit count unchanged.

    What 800 means for the plan

    A training game at 800 look-aheads costs four times as much as at 200. Measured on one Modal L4 (9 Nov): one move for 100 games at once takes 0.92 seconds, so 758–1,137 games per hour and $0.98–1.47 per 1,000 games, in line with the Phase 3 estimate. That is a best case: an untrained network's lines go only about 4 moves deep, and a trained one digs deeper, which makes every look-ahead slower. Measured on the same GPU with move probabilities set by hand: $1.15–1.73 per 1,000 games at 8 moves deep, $1.63–2.45 at 19, and $1.98–2.98 at 25. A rough count on the best case: the money set aside buys about 7,000–10,000 games per training run instead of 27,000–40,000 at 200. That is under the plan's 15,000 line, below which Justin's PC runs seeds 4 and 5. To be settled before the training runs (Phase 7); a Stanford cluster may become available.

    Phase 1 · approved 8 Nov 2026

    The plan, frozen before any results

    Three ways to start the same network. Same code, same settings, same evaluation; only the starting weights differ. Each is trained five times with different seeds.

    Random start

    Standard random weights. The baseline that AlphaZero itself used.

    Claude writes the weights

    Claude sets every weight directly, with no training. It may test its network up to 50 times against practice bots or on positions it picks.

    Claude writes a heuristic

    The control. Claude writes its chess knowledge as a Python evaluator instead, and the network is trained to copy it before self-play begins.

    Network
    6 residual blocks × 64 filters, batch norm as in AlphaZero (restored 8 Nov), 571,276 weights
    Search
    800 simulations per move as in AlphaZero (raised from 200, 8 Nov), AlphaZero’s exploration formula
    Headline measure
    Self-play games needed to reach the finish line
    Finish line
    Score 50% against a fixed Stockfish level, set by a calibration run
    Verdict
    One-sided Mann–Whitney test, 5 runs against 5, at p < 0.05
    Compute
    Modal GPUs, planned at $192 or less of a $240 cap
